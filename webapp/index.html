Aerial Photography with UAVs

From history and UAV types to geodesy, GNSS, and mission planning

This course is based on the course of Artem Mazurok (St Petersburg University). It was made available again after being removed from coursera.org. Here you will find structured material on aerial photography using unmanned aerial vehicles—with clear explanations and visuals.

INTRODUCTION

What is Aerial Photography?

Aerial photography is photographing the Earth’s surface from an aircraft—here, an unmanned one—to obtain objective spatial characteristics and interpret them.

Requirements include a defined overlap between images, accuracy for photo centers, and other technical and procedural conditions that we will cover in the following modules.

Boston, as the Eagle and the Wild Goose See It, 1860. Early aerial photography.

Brief history

  • 1851: wet colloidal process replaces daguerreotype
  • 1858: first air photo from a balloon (Nadar, Paris), not preserved
  • 1860: first preserved aerial photo (Boston)
  • 1910: International Photogrammetric Society created
  • 2000: first digital aerial camera (Leica DMC I)
  • Today: UAVs account for ~half of aerial photography work
WEEK 1

UAVs: Types, Construction & Payload

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles are defined as aircraft flying without a pilot on board, controlled automatically and/or by an operator from the ground.

Classification by weight & type

Two broad categories: lighter than air (airships, balloons) and heavier than air. In Russia: <250 g (no registration), 250 g–30 kg (registration), >30 kg (registration + airworthiness certificate).

Heavier-than-air UAVs
  • Fixed-wing — airplane-like, long endurance, needs take-off/landing space
  • Multirotor — quad/hex/octocopter; hover, small landing area; shorter flight time
  • Hybrid (VTOL) — vertical take-off + fixed-wing cruise; more complex, higher cost

Fixed-wing (Sovzond Wing X5) — long endurance, needs runway or catapult launch.

Multirotor (DJI Inspire 2 + military MQ-8B) — hover, small footprint, shorter range.

Hybrid VTOL (YUANJET TA-10) — vertical take-off, then transitions to fixed-wing cruise.

Construction

Every UAV has structural parts (wings, propellers, body) and on-board electronics: autopilot, navigation, payload. The autopilot estimates position and motion, controls propulsion, and communicates with the ground station. Navigation combines an IMU (gyroscopes, accelerometers, magnetometers) with a satellite receiver (GNSS).

Left: MEMS accelerometer at microscale. Right: Sensonor STIM210 — the kind of IMU inside a UAV autopilot.

Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) combines three-axis gyroscopes, accelerometers, and often magnetometers on a single chip. Together with a GNSS receiver, the autopilot fuses these signals to estimate position, velocity, and attitude at high frequency (100–1 000 Hz), even when satellite signals are momentarily lost.

Payload types

Flight payload: equipment for autonomy and safety (e.g. pitot tube, ultrasonic/infrared rangefinders). Mounted payload: sensors for the mission—cameras, lidars, etc.

By sensing method:

  • Passive — RGB, NIR, thermal, hyperspectral cameras; magnetometers
  • Active — LiDAR (light detection and ranging), radar

RGB cameras are the most common (orthomosaics, 3D models). NIR supports vegetation indices. LiDAR can “see” under foliage via multiple returns per pulse; it is more expensive and heavier than photogrammetry.

Full-frame Sony RX-1 RGB camera — used for orthomosaics, 3D models, and high-res mapping.

Resonon Pika XC hyperspectral camera (up to 450 bands) — geological surveys and environmental monitoring.

DJI Phantom 4 — compact quadcopter for consumer and survey use.

Geoscan-201 — fixed-wing platform for large-area mapping missions.

WEEK 2

Coordinate Systems & Geodetic Control

Survey results must be tied to a coordinate system and vertical datum so that orthomosaics, DEMs, and plans match existing maps and documentation.

Coordinate systems

Global: geocentric (e.g. WGS-84, PZ-90), origin at Earth’s mass center; Z to pole, X to equator/prime meridian. Reference: national systems on a reference ellipsoid (e.g. GSK-2011 in Russia, replacing SK-42/SK-95). Local: for a region (e.g. a subject of the federation), with defined transformation keys to the state system.

Geocentric frame (e.g. WGS-84)

Planar coordinates often use Gauss–Krüger (Russia) or UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator). Heights: normal heights from quasigeoid (e.g. Baltic 1977 in Russia) or geodetic heights from ellipsoid.

Vertical datums: the earth surface, quasigeoid, geoid, and reference ellipsoid — and how normal, orthometric, and geodetic heights relate to each other.

Triangulation network — the classical method for establishing geodetic control across a region before GPS. Points at vertices are measured and adjusted as a network.

GNSS in brief

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) provide position by measuring distances to satellites. The control segment maintains orbits and clocks; the user segment includes consumer devices and geodetic receivers (single- or dual-frequency, used for surveying and UAV positioning).

Absolute positioning (one receiver): ~several metres. Differential/relative (base + rover): centimetre-level. Methods include code and carrier-phase measurements; phase requires fixing integer ambiguities.

Key terms

RTK — real-time kinematic; base sends corrections to rover live. PPK — post-processing kinematic; corrections applied after flight. PPP — precise point positioning; single receiver + precise orbits/clocks, no base.

Geodetic control for aerial survey

Two main approaches:

Ground control points (GCPs)

Mark 3+ identifiable points, measure their coordinates (e.g. by GNSS), use them in photogrammetric adjustment. Pros: lower cost, simple. Cons: slower, not ideal for large or inaccessible areas.

Onboard geodetic GNSS + base / PPP

UAV carries a survey-grade receiver; use a base station (e.g. <30 km) for differential (PPK/RTK) or PPP corrections. Pros: high accuracy, less field time, suitable for large areas. Cons: higher equipment cost; base or correction service needed (unless using PPP).

For plan conformity and accuracy control, coordinates/heights from the orthomosaic or DEM are compared with independent geodetic measurements at check points; standard errors are evaluated against the required scale.

WEEK 3

Requirements, Reconnaissance & Ground Control Station

The technical task defines scale, overlap, resolution, accuracy, equipment, and deadlines. Preparatory work includes permissions, geodetic support, and flight mission design.

Aerial photography requirements

Key parameters: scale, overlap (along and across track), ground sampling distance (GSD), horizontal and vertical accuracy. For a 1:2000 topographic plan, orthomosaic resolution is typically at least ~10 cm/pixel (often 8–9 cm for margin). Flight altitude is set from GSD and camera parameters. Terrain (e.g. SRTM, ASTER DEM) is used in mission planning to keep constant GSD and avoid obstacles.

Preparatory steps (example: small city 30 km²)

  • Obtain permissions (airspace, FSB, administration, etc.) and check restricted zones (e.g. fpln.ru, KML in Google Earth).
  • Request SGN (state geodetic network) point coordinates/heights from Rosreestr; check for permanent base stations.
  • Choose suitable days (no low clouds, no precipitation, sun height ≥20°, no snow if needed).
  • Build flight mission: area, GSD, overlap, take-off/landing; use DEM for terrain-following if needed.
  • Place or select GCPs (e.g. road markings in cities); if using onboard GNSS, GCPs can serve as check points only.

Ground control station (GCS)

The GCS is the hardware–software complex for controlling the UAV, receiving telemetry, and viewing video. It can be ultralight (smartphone/tablet + remote, e.g. DJI), light (laptop + radio, industrial software like Geoscan Planner, UgCS), or stationary (military-style cabins).

Main functions: create flight mission (routes, altitude, overlap, camera trigger), control during flight (upload mission, monitor status, change task or land if needed), and assess survey quality (photo coverage, GNSS availability) before leaving the field.

Typical workflow
Plan & permissions → Flight mission → Fly & capture → Process & deliver

WEEK 4

GNSS Data Processing

Surveying and UAV missions often rely on post-processing of GNSS data: static (networks, GCPs) and kinematic (trajectory, photo positions).

Static processing

Used for geodetic networks and GCP coordinate determination. Data are stored in exchange formats (e.g. RINEX). Processing steps: import, set antenna height and model, cutoff angle and SNR mask, then baseline computation (double-difference phase, ambiguity resolution). Multiple baselines are combined in a network adjustment. Software: Trimble Business Center, Leica Geo Office, Topcon Magnet Tools, RTKLib (free).

RINEX (Receiver Independent Exchange Format) v3 file header — the standard format for exchanging raw GNSS observation data between different receiver brands and processing software.

RINEX key fields:

  • Receiver type, antenna model and height
  • Approximate position (XYZ)
  • Observation types recorded (L1, L2, C1, P1…)
  • Sampling interval and time of first observation
  • Per-epoch: satellite IDs, pseudoranges, carrier phases, Doppler, signal strength

Kinematic processing

Used for moving receivers: trajectory and event positions (e.g. camera trigger times). Requires higher recording rate (e.g. 5–10 Hz). Rover is initialised (static or on-the-fly); base and rover data must overlap in time without gaps. Processing computes the baseline at each epoch and often applies a Kalman filter for smooth trajectory. Forward and backward runs can be combined. Output: trajectory and event coordinates (e.g. for photogrammetry). Software: Topcon Magnet Office, Javad Justin, Novatel GrafNav.

RTK and PPP

Real-time kinematic (RTK): base sends corrections to the rover in real time (radio or cellular); centimetre accuracy within ~30 km of the base. Post-processing kinematic (PPK): same idea, corrections applied after the flight. Precise Point Positioning (PPP): single receiver with precise orbits and clock corrections (e.g. from IGS); no base station, but convergence takes time and final products may be delayed (e.g. 2 weeks for final ephemeris).

SPECIAL TOPIC

Drone Innovation & the War in Ukraine

The conflict in Ukraine has become the most intense drone war in history, compressing years of UAV development into months and reshaping how the world thinks about unmanned systems.

Both sides operate hundreds of thousands of drones per year. The war has driven rapid innovation across FPV kamikaze drones, loitering munitions, electronic warfare, AI-guided navigation, and naval surface drones—with direct implications for civilian UAV technology.

From hobbyist to weapon: FPV drones

FPVKamikazeCOTS adaptation

Racing FPV (first-person view) drones were adapted into one-way attack systems. Pilots wear FPV goggles and fly the drone into a target at high speed. Key characteristics:

  • Cost: ~$400–800 to build; cheap enough to saturate defences at scale.
  • Speed & agility: up to 200 km/h, hard to intercept with conventional systems.
  • Payload: typically a 40 mm or 60 mm grenade, RPG warhead, or custom munition.
  • Training: pilots trained in weeks using simulators; Ukraine has trained tens of thousands.
  • Limitation: range limited by analogue video link (~5–10 km); vulnerable to jamming and skilled EW.

Ukraine industrialised domestic FPV production, reducing foreign dependency and cutting lead times from months to days. Russia followed suit, dramatically scaling up its own FPV output through 2023–2024.

Loitering munitions

Loitering munitionLancetShahed-136Switchblade

Unlike FPV drones, loitering munitions can orbit an area and choose when to strike, often with optical or radar seekers.

Russia — Lancet / KUB

The Lancet-3 loitering munition has been highly effective against Ukrainian artillery and armoured vehicles. Uses optical/TV guidance; range ~40 km; warhead 3–5 kg. Produced in large numbers domestically.

Russia — Shahed-136 (Geran-2)

Iranian-designed delta-wing drone, ~2 m span, 50 kg warhead, range >1 000 km; used in mass salvos to overwhelm air defence. Ukraine produces Palianytsia and similar designs in response.

Ukraine — Switchblade (US-supplied)

Switchblade 300: anti-personnel, ~10 min loiter, 10 km range. Switchblade 600: anti-armour, ~40 min, ~40 km range. Tube-launched, backpack-portable.

Ukraine — domestic strike UAVs

Ukraine developed several long-range jet-propelled drones (e.g. UJ-22, Beaver/Bobr) capable of striking targets inside Russia. Some have reached Moscow. Declared range 800–1 000+ km.

Commercial drones on the battlefield

DJI MavicReconnaissanceArtillery correction

Off-the-shelf commercial drones — primarily DJI Mavic 3, Autel EVO — are the backbone of frontline reconnaissance. Operators use them to observe enemy positions, correct artillery fire in real time, and drop small munitions (grenades, modified mortar rounds) with 3D-printed holders.

This mainstreamed the role of the UAV operator as a standard infantry skill. DJI eventually geofenced the conflict zone; both sides turned to non-DJI hardware or modified firmware to bypass restrictions. The conflict accelerated demand for non-Chinese, non-geofenced platforms.

Electronic warfare (EW) & GPS-denied navigation

EWGPS jammingSpoofingOptical navigation

Russia deployed dense, layered EW coverage — GPS jammers, spoofing systems (e.g. Krasuha, Tirada-2S), and video-link jammers — making the frontline one of the most GPS-degraded environments ever encountered. This drove rapid innovation:

  • Inertial navigation & terrain matching: drones cross-reference onboard IMU with stored terrain maps.
  • Computer vision / optical flow: cameras provide velocity and position estimates independent of GNSS, enabling stable hover in GPS-denied zones.
  • Visual odometry: sequences of camera frames used to estimate trajectory without satellite signals.
  • Fibre-optic guided FPV: Ukraine developed fibre-optic tethered FPV drones immune to RF jamming; the drone unreels a thin fibre as it flies, transmitting video and receiving controls through the cable.
  • Frequency hopping & spread spectrum: data links adapted to jump frequencies faster than jamming systems can follow.
Layered EW environment — GPS-denied battlespace

AI, autonomy & target recognition

AI guidanceAutonomous terminalMachine vision

As jamming made remote-piloted final approaches unreliable, both sides began integrating AI-powered terminal guidance. The drone is piloted to the vicinity of a target, then an onboard neural network locks onto a vehicle or object and steers autonomously to impact — even with the data link severed.

Ukraine's Saker Scout and several Lancet variants reportedly use this capability. This marks a significant shift: the human operator makes the targeting decision, but the drone executes autonomously. It raises important legal and ethical questions about meaningful human control that the international community is still debating.

AI is also used for post-flight damage assessment — automatically analysing reconnaissance footage to detect and classify destroyed or damaged equipment.

Uncrewed surface vehicles (USVs) — naval drones

USVMaritimeSea Baby

Ukraine extended the drone war to the Black Sea. Uncrewed surface vehicles — essentially fast boats loaded with explosives — successfully struck Russian warships, a feat previously undemonstrated in warfare at this scale.

  • Sea Baby / Magura V5: Ukraine's primary USV; ~200 kg warhead, ~400 km range, GPS + optical guidance; used in attacks on the Kerch Bridge and Russian Black Sea Fleet vessels.
  • Forced the Russian fleet to abandon Sevastopol as a primary base, reshaping Black Sea strategic balance.
  • Demonstrated that inexpensive autonomous platforms can threaten multi-billion-dollar warships, accelerating naval drone programmes worldwide.

Counter-drone systems

C-UASKineticEW interceptLaser

The scale of drone use drove equally rapid development of counter-UAS (C-UAS) technology:

Electronic

GPS jammers, video-link jammers, spoofers. Cheap and effective against unprotected consumer drones. Limitations: affects friendly systems; FPV drones on fibres are immune.

Kinetic interceptors

Modified anti-aircraft guns (ZU-23-2), shotgun-armed soldiers, dedicated gun systems (Gepard). Expensive per kill against cheap FPV drones; effective against larger targets.

Net guns & drone catchers

Handheld launchers fire entanglement nets; effective at short range for protecting high-value static assets. Low cost.

Directed energy (lasers)

Ukraine and allies have begun fielding laser systems capable of burning drone optics or structure. High cost, limited range, but effectively unlimited magazine and low per-shot cost.

No single solution dominates; effective C-UAS requires layered, heterogeneous systems — the same lesson that applies to air defence at scale.

Key takeaways for civilian UAV technology

The conflict has had measurable spillover effects on civilian and commercial drone development:

  • Resilient navigation: GPS-denied operation techniques (visual odometry, terrain matching) are now mainstream R&D priorities for delivery and inspection drones.
  • Anti-jamming data links: frequency-hopping and encrypted links are becoming standard rather than optional.
  • Domestic supply chains: many countries have recognised the strategic risk of over-reliance on Chinese-made drone components and are building local manufacturing capacity.
  • Swarm coordination: low-cost distributed sensing — one drone spots, another strikes — informs civilian applications in search-and-rescue and precision agriculture.
  • Rapid iteration culture: Ukraine's model of designing, printing, testing, and deploying new drone variants in days is influencing agile manufacturing practices in civilian aerospace.

Brief timeline

  • Feb 2022 — Bayraktar TB2 becomes a symbol of Ukrainian resistance; destroys Russian armoured columns in the north.
  • Spring 2022 — DJI Mavics ubiquitous for artillery spotting; grenade-drop modifications appear within weeks.
  • Autumn 2022 — Russia begins mass Shahed-136 salvos against Ukrainian infrastructure; Ukraine adapts with layered air defence.
  • 2023 — FPV drone production industrialises on both sides; Ukrainian USVs score hits on Black Sea Fleet; Lancet loitering munition proliferates on Russian side.
  • 2023–2024 — Fibre-optic FPV drones deployed; AI-guided terminal homing enters use; drone pilot training becomes mass-scale.
  • 2024 — Ukraine claims >1 million drone strikes in the year; long-range Ukrainian drones reach targets 1 000+ km inside Russia.
  • Ongoing — Both sides race to develop anti-drone laser systems, autonomous swarms, and next-generation EW countermeasures.
SUMMARY

Key takeaways

  • Aerial photography from UAVs requires defined overlap, GSD, and geodetic control to produce orthomosaics and DEMs that match specifications.
  • UAV choice: fixed-wing for large areas and endurance; multirotor for hover and 3D modelling; hybrid for VTOL + range.
  • Payload: RGB/NIR/thermal and LiDAR are complementary; photogrammetry is cheaper, LiDAR excels under vegetation.
  • Coordinates & heights: bind results to the required system (state or local) and vertical datum (e.g. Baltic 77) and check accuracy with independent points.
  • Geodetic control: GCPs for small or low-budget projects; onboard geodetic GNSS + base or PPP for large or high-accuracy work.
  • Mission planning: permissions, SGN points, DEM-based routes, GSD and overlap; GCS for mission creation, flight control, and quality check.
  • GNSS processing: static for networks and GCPs; kinematic/PPK for UAV trajectory and photo positions; RTK for real-time; PPP when no base is available.
  • War in Ukraine: the conflict has been the fastest-ever forcing function for drone innovation—FPV kamikazes, loitering munitions, GPS-denied AI guidance, naval USVs, and layered C-UAS have reshaped the field and are already feeding back into civilian UAV R&D.
